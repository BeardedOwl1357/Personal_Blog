# Inventory management

## Definition / Formula

**TL;DR:** measuring and reducing wastage**. If the increase is instead driven by higher market prices, the restaurant can respond through **ingredient substitution, selective price or portion changes, and recipe/product optimization**. The key objective is simple: **generate the same revenue while using fewer resources or getting more revenue from every rupee spent on raw materials.**


## Explanation

Imagine a restaurant whose revenue this year is almost the same as last year, but its profitability has fallen. Since Revenue = Quantity Sold × Average Selling Price, if neither quantity sold nor average selling price has changed meaningfully, the next question is whether the restaurant has become less efficient at converting raw materials into food that is actually sold. Raw-material consumption can broadly be divided into Recipe/Portion Usage + Wastage + Other Uses. Wastage itself can come from expiry, preparation errors, and overproduction. Overproduction is particularly relevant in restaurants because food is often prepared before customers arrive. For example, if 100 portions are prepared but only 80 are sold, the remaining 20 portions represent potential waste. Therefore, the objective of inventory management is not simply to buy cheaper ingredients, but to buy, store and use the right quantity of ingredients with minimum waste.

This can be achieved through three levers. First, better demand forecasting helps the restaurant estimate customer demand and prepare or stock only what is required, with a reasonable safety margin. Second, stricter inventory controls reduce spoilage. Techniques such as FIFO (First In, First Out) ensure older inventory is used first, while FEFO (First Expiry, First Out) prioritizes ingredients that will expire sooner. Third, wastage measurement helps identify where and why waste occurs. For example, tracking waste by outlet, ingredient, dish or reason can reveal that one restaurant store consistently wastes more vegetables than others, pointing to problems in forecasting, preparation or storage.

However, raw-material costs can increase even when the restaurant's own processes are efficient. Raw materials can broadly come from sources such as agriculture, dairy, poultry and mining, and the final purchase price can be viewed as Market Price + Supplier Premium/Discount. Market prices are influenced by factors such as global demand and supply, farmer input costs, exchange rates and inflation. The supplier premium or discount, meanwhile, depends on factors such as purchase volume and bargaining power. Larger volumes can create economies of scale, while bargaining power increases when the restaurant has multiple alternative suppliers and switching between them is relatively easy. This distinction is important because it separates factors the restaurant can control from those it cannot.

If the market price of an ingredient rises, the restaurant has three broad strategic responses. It can substitute ingredients where a lower-cost alternative can provide a similar customer experience. It can use selective price increases or portion changes for dishes that are particularly affected by input-cost inflation, rather than increasing prices across the entire menu. Finally, it can pursue recipe and product optimization, reducing the quantity or cost of ingredients without materially affecting the customer's perception of value.

Ultimately, inventory management is about one question: How can a restaurant generate the same revenue while extracting more value from every rupee spent on raw materials? This makes inventory management more than a back-end operational activity. It is a direct link between customer demand, procurement, kitchen operations, and profitability.
