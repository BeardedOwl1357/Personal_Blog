# Anchoring effect

## Definition / Formula

Rs. 1,500 feels cheap when we see "Rs. 3,000 → Rs. 1,500," even though Rs. 1,500 might not feel cheap if shown by itself.

## Explanation

Discounts exploit several psychological biases. A discounted product can feel like a bargain because we compare its sale price with the artificially prominent original price. This is called the **anchoring effect**. For example, Rs. 1,500 feels cheap when we see "Rs. 3,000 → Rs. 1,500," even though Rs. 1,500 might not feel cheap if shown by itself.

Discounts also create **urgency and fear of missing out**. Phrases like "50% off," "today only," or "last 3 left" shift our thinking from "Do I need this?" to "Can I afford to miss this opportunity?" This can make us buy things we otherwise wouldn't.

The biggest mistake is treating money not spent as money "saved." If you buy an unnecessary Rs. 1,500 item because it was 50% off, you haven't saved Rs. 1,500. You have **spent Rs. 1,500**. A discount is genuine savings only when you already intended to buy the item and get it for less than you expected to pay.

A useful rule is therefore: **Don't ask, "How much am I saving?" Ask, "Would I buy this if there were no discount?"** If the answer is no, the discount may be creating the desire rather than satisfying an existing need.
