# I don't particularly hate it

## Definition / Formula

The value of an experience isn't necessarily the enjoyment you derive from its intended purpose. It can also be the unexpected interests, observations and connections it generates.  A restaurant outing can become an interesting conversation. If you only pursue things you're already motivated to do, you risk systematically excluding experiences whose appeal you haven't discovered yet. You don't need to want an experience before trying it. Sometimes you need to experience it before knowing whether you want it.

## Explanation

My reluctance to experiencing new things is in the following forms
- No motivation to experience it
- No idea about what to do / what is expected / feeling too conscious

I can cure the first point short term (or maybe long term as well) by being around with people who want to do the thing and I join them because I want to do it with them. For example, I have no inherent motivation to go to concerts or watch cricket match however if I am with someone who is interested, accompanying them gives me a chance to discover whether I really like the activity or not. Since most first or second experiences are not reliable judgements of an experience so the limit should be around 5-6. If, after 5-6 experiences, I don't like it then I can make an opinion that "I don't like this because of XYZ"

> The value of an experience isn't necessarily the enjoyment you derive from its intended purpose. It can also be the unexpected interests, observations and connections it generates.  A restaurant outing can become an interesting conversation. A cricket match can teach you about game strategy. A trip can make you curious about architecture, geography or local economics.  You don't have to enjoy the conventional selling point of an activity to extract something meaningful from it

I have realised that if I stop expecting "really feeling joy" from the moment and just try to experience it like those who actually enjoy, I seem to enjoy it as well. This happened at a concert where I inherently had no idea of what to do except just standing. However, I saw people doing the following things
- Recording videos which they would probably upload on social media
- Singing the lyrics of the songs they knew
- Smoking / getting high
- Dancing (some were dancing mildly and some were wildly)

I don't smoke so I crossed out that thing. I tried doing the other things whenever I could and coupled with the expectation that "I don't really need to enjoy it compared to other things I really enjoy", I was able to detach from evaluating the experience and just be in the moment. In the process, I saw that I am really curious as to how sound acoustics work in the stage, what are the carbon emissions of a concert , how they use drones and how they use lighting effects. Just by staying there and managing my expectations, I was able to convert a "usually bad" experience into an "interesting" experience. This is kinda like fake it till you make it but in a weird way I guess. 

> Copying behaviour doesn't mean you have to manufacture emotions. You can sing without feeling ecstatic, dance without feeling uninhibited, and attend without pretending to be fascinated. Your objective is participation, not convincing yourself that you're having the time of your life.

However, when I tried to share this, I was questioned "if you don't really enjoy it, why do you even attend the event". Fair question. I tried to explain my reasoning but gave up because my thoughts weren't concrete. However, at this moment, I have a faint idea. I am generally not averse to trying most things. I just neither have an intrinsic pull nor an idea of what to do / expect. 

Chatgpt apparently gave me a good explanation. I was struggling to explain this because I was trying to justify attendance through enjoyment. But enjoyment is only one possible reason. There are at least four reasons where one might attend something they don't initially like

| Reason                  | What you gain                                 |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Exploration             | Discover whether you like something           |
| Social participation    | Spend time with people you value              |
| Knowledge               | Learn how an unfamiliar activity works        |
| Behavioural familiarity | Learn how to participate without feeling lost |

> If you only pursue things you're already motivated to do, you risk systematically excluding experiences whose appeal you haven't discovered yet. You don't need to want an experience before trying it. Sometimes you need to experience it before knowing whether you want it.

If I experience new things, I expand my knowledge. The benefit of me expanding my knowledge is to get a sense of what I like and don't. Based on this, I can decide what kind of life I want to build and how / why I behave the way I do. Also, something from an exterior way can be misleading from the way it actually works. For example, trying various restaurants looks like just going, eating, clicking pics and uploading in social media. However, when one goes with friends, various conversations and discussions follow, multiple dishes can be split and tasted and perhaps after the event, some new plan springs into action. 

So for me, I think I am now in a stage where I can try new things that seem "bother" initially but I am not totally repulsed by it through
1. Finding people who already like the thing
2. Getting a rough idea of what to expect and what people usually do in it
3. Actually doing it for about 5-6 times and thinking about what I liked and didn't. During the process, I try to copy others and fake enjoyment. 
4. Then coming to a consensus whether I want to do this more times or not. A side advantage I get is that if I am ever forced to participate in the activity again, I know how to act. 


A thinking structure that I can use while evaluating my experience with an activity can be
1. Did I enjoy the activity?
2. Did I enjoy the circumstances in which I experienced it?
3. Did I discover something interesting despite not enjoying the activity?
